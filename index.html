// summary

The essentials in 30 seconds

The EU AI Act is the world's first comprehensive AI regulation. It entered into force on August 1, 2024, and is being phased in through 2027. It applies to everyone who develops, deploys, or distributes AI systems in the EU — including non-EU companies if their system's output is used within EU territory.

// tl;dr
  • Risk-based approach: 4 categories, from unacceptable to minimal
  • Providers (developers) bear the majority of obligations
  • Deployers (professional users) also have responsibilities
  • GPAI models (general-purpose AI, e.g. LLMs) have specific rules
  • Extraterritorial scope: if the output is used in the EU, you're covered
  • Fines up to €40M or 7% of global annual turnover
  • Partial exemptions for open source (under conditions)
// classification

The 4 risk levels

The regulation classifies each AI system according to the potential danger it poses to fundamental rights, safety, and the health of EU citizens. The higher the risk, the heavier the obligations.

⛔
Banned since Feb. 2025
Unacceptable Risk

AI systems completely banned as they conflict with EU values. 8 prohibited practices, including subliminal manipulation, government social scoring, real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces (with limited exceptions), and emotion recognition in the workplace or schools.

Examples: Social scoring, untargeted facial scraping, sensitive biometric categorisation
⚠️
Applicable Aug. 2026 / 2027
High Risk

Systems subject to the strictest requirements: conformity assessment, technical documentation, risk management, data governance, human oversight, robustness, and cybersecurity. Covers domains defined in Annex III of the regulation.

Domains: biometrics, critical infrastructure, education, employment & HR, credit/insurance access, justice, law enforcement, immigration
💬
Transparency obligations
Limited Risk

AI systems that interact directly with users. Main obligation: clearly inform the user they are interacting with AI. GPAI (general-purpose AI) models fall into this category with additional specific obligations.

Examples: chatbots, deepfake generators, conversational systems
✅
No obligations
Minimal Risk

The vast majority of current AI applications. No regulatory obligations, though voluntary codes of conduct are encouraged. Note: a system can change category if its use evolves.

Examples: spam filters, AI-powered video games, content recommendations
Open source note: Models released under a free and open-source license benefit from partial exemptions (no full technical documentation, no training data summary required) — unless the model is classified as posing systemic risk, is monetised, or the system is classified as high-risk or prohibited. Transparency obligations (Art. 50) apply in all cases.
// schedule

Implementation timeline

The regulation is being applied progressively. Here are the key dates to plan your compliance roadmap.

August 1, 2024
Entry into force

Published in the Official Journal of the EU and countdown begins.

February 2, 2025
Prohibitions + AI literacy obligation

The 8 prohibited practices become effective. Organisations must ensure sufficient AI literacy among their staff.

August 2, 2025
GPAI obligations + governance

Rules for providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models enter into application. Establishment of the AI Office, AI Board, and designation of national competent authorities.

August 2, 2026
General application

All remaining provisions become applicable, including high-risk systems (Annex III), transparency obligations, and Commission enforcement powers (GPAI fines).

August 2, 2027
Regulated products + existing GPAI deadline

High-risk AI systems embedded in regulated products (medical devices, machinery, etc.) must be compliant. GPAI models already on the market before August 2025 must achieve compliance.

Obligations by role

The AI Act distinguishes between providers (those who develop the system) and deployers (those who use it professionally). Providers bear the heaviest compliance burden.

Provider (developer)

  • Quality management system
  • Comprehensive technical documentation
  • Conformity assessment (high-risk)
  • Risk management throughout the lifecycle
  • Training data governance and quality
  • Automatic operation logging
  • Human oversight by design
  • Accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity
  • Registration in the EU database
  • Post-market monitoring
  • Serious incident reporting

Deployer (professional user)

  • Use in accordance with provider instructions
  • Effective human oversight
  • System operation monitoring
  • Retention of automatically generated logs
  • Fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA)
  • Informing affected individuals
  • Serious incident reporting

GPAI — all providers

  • Model technical documentation
  • Usage instructions for downstream providers
  • Copyright compliance policy
  • Public summary of training data

GPAI — systemic risk

  • In-depth model evaluations
  • Adversarial testing (red teaming)
  • Systemic risk assessment and mitigation
  • Enhanced cybersecurity protection
  • Serious incident reporting to the AI Office
  • Notification within 2 weeks of reaching 10²⁵ FLOPs threshold
pseudo-code
// Determine your role and obligations if (you.develop(aiSystem) && you.placeOnMarket(EU)) { role = "provider"; // Maximum obligations } if (you.useInProContext(aiSystem) && scope.includes(EU)) { role = "deployer"; // Lighter but real obligations } if (you.modify(gpaiModel, "substantially")) { role = "provider"; // You inherit provider obligations } if (model.flops >= 1e25) { risk = "systemic"; // Enhanced obligations + AI Office notification }

Developer checklist

6 concrete steps to get compliant, whether you're a provider or deployer.

01
Inventory your AI systems

Catalogue all current and planned AI systems, including third-party APIs, internal tools, and integrated open source models.

02
Classify the risk level

Assess each system against Annex III (high-risk domains) and Article 5 (prohibitions). Watch out for grey areas — consult legal counsel if necessary.

03
Determine your role

Are you a provider, deployer, importer, or distributor? Significant fine-tuning of a GPAI model can reclassify you as a provider.

04
Audit your current practices

Compare your existing processes against Articles 9–15: logging, human oversight, data governance, technical documentation.

05
Review contracts and the value chain

Update contracts with your AI vendors, clarifying who is responsible for what. Check the licenses of any open source models you use.

06
Establish AI governance

Create a governance framework (or adapt your existing one): AI policy, system registry, post-market monitoring process, AI literacy training plan for your teams.
